A White former high school psychologist failed to show that she suffered discrimination on the basis of her race and disability when, in part, a co-worker allegedly referred to her as “Karen,” a federal judge held in an Oct. 20 decision.

The incident cited in D’Aiello v. Freeport High Schools occurred in 2021, when the plaintiff claimed that she and another White employee were greeted by an assistant principal’s secretary, who said: “Here comes Karen 1 and Karen 2.” The plaintiff understood “Karen” to be a derogatory term for White women and raised the issue with leadership.

Per court documents, a meeting was held afterward in which the secretary allegedly mimicked the plaintiff’s way of speaking and mannerisms, which the plaintiff perceived to be related to her disability. The plaintiff asked the secretary to stop, which she did. The secretary also apologized for the “Karen” remark.

As the meeting progressed, however, parties to the conversation expressed disagreement about whether the term represented a racial remark. At that point, the meeting “began to devolve.”

The plaintiff later filed a Title VII complaint against school officials and asked Freeport School District, in New York, to follow up on name-calling and aggression but did not check a box on the complaint intake form indicating that race formed a basis of her complaint.

The district conducted an investigation and issued a report concluding that the conduct did not meet the criteria for harassment.

The plaintiff resigned in 2024, stating in a letter to the school that she felt ignored and dismissed by leadership and claiming she had been mistreated by both the secretary and the assistant principal for whom the secretary worked. By that time, she had already filed a lawsuit against the defendants alleging discrimination under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act, which protects employees from discrimination, including based on race or color; the Americans with Disabilities Act; and New York state laws.

The court granted summary judgment to the defendants on the plaintiff’s federal claims and declined to address her state claims.

Specifically, Judge Joan Azrack of the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York wrote that the Title VII claim failed as a matter of law because she could not identify any adverse action that occurred as a result of the alleged conduct.

The plaintiff cited harm such as emotional distress and expenses for medication and leave. But such effects do not themselves constitute actionable adverse actions under the harm standard established by the U.S. Supreme Court in the 2023 decision Muldrow v. City of St. Louis, in which the high court said that plaintiffs must show “some harm” regarding employment as a result of discriminatory action, Azrack held. The Muldrow decision eliminated federal courts’ elevated standards requiring discrimination plaintiffs to show “significant” harm from alleged discriminatory conduct.

The judge added that the “Karen” comment, along with similar remarks cited by the plaintiff, were not adverse actions because they did not materially affect her employment. Similarly, the judge held that the plaintiff failed to present evidence that the district did not conduct a proper investigation into the behavior.

“Although the remarks may have been ‘unpleasant’ to her, that alone does not constitute a materially disadvantageous change in employment sufficient to qualify as an adverse action,” Azrack said.

#7112. Special education disputes continue to rise

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Written state complaints increased while mediation requests hit an all-time high, a CADRE analysis of 2024-25 data shows.

The number of special education state complaints has continued its year-over-year climb nationwide, jumping 7% between the 2023-24 and 2024-25 school years alone, according to an analysis released this month by the Center for Appropriate Dispute Resolution in Special Education, a federally funded technical assistance center. Between 2014 and 2024, the tally skyrocketed by 113%.

Formally called "written state complaints," they often trigger state-led investigations into special education-related concerns brought forward by anyone. Some 10,610 written state complaints were filed during the 2024-25 school year, up from 9,927 the year before. Just three states — Texas, Massachusetts and California — accounted for 26% of the nation’s written state complaints in 2024-25. CADRE’s analysis is based on data collected by the U.S. Department of Education and also from data CADRE collected directly from states.

Written state complaints are just one course of action in a continuum of special education dispute procedures and practices, according to CADRE. Other potential practices under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act include due process complaints and mediation. CADRE's continuum also includes dispute prevention approaches such as family engagement.

The recent CADRE data analysis doesn’t explore the causes for trends in dispute activities. However, CADRE Director Melanie Reese said in an Oct. 21 email that the 2024-25 data shows families and schools are using IDEA dispute resolution "more than ever."

CADRE's finding that written state complaints are increasing aligns with trends seen by the Council for Exceptional Children and the National Association of State Directors of Special Education through state surveys.

CEC and NASDSE, for example, found that survey responses from 35 states administered this year showed a 56% increase in special education written state complaints between the 2023-24 and 2025-26 school years.

While CEC and NASDSE stress that there is no single reason for this increase, they said many families have reported a lack of faith that the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights will resolve their concerns. The Education Department significantly reduced OCR's workforce last year — after which it did begin rehiring some staff. Earlier this year, the agency began outsourcing some OCR functions to the U.S. Department of Justice as part of the Trump administration's broader effort to wind down the Education Department.

OCR has had 100 K-12 resolution agreements for disability discrimination complaints so far this year, according to its online resolution database.

Between March and September 2025, OCR resolved 7,072 of more than 9,000 complaints received of all types, mostly by dismissing 90% of them, according to a U.S. Government Accountability Office report released in January. As with written state complaints, anyone can file an OCR complaint at no cost.

States are required to address special education written state complaints within 60 days of receiving them. More than three-quarters —  78% — of the 2024-25 complaints were resolved within that time frame, according to the CADRE analysis. However, that was down from the 92% average between 2014-15 and 2022-23.

According to CEC and NASDSE, state and local special education staff shortages and turnover are making it extremely difficult to provide timely resolutions.

Additionally, the number of students qualifying for services under IDEA has increased in recent years. About 15% of the student population in 2023-24, qualified for IDEA services, according to data from the U.S. Department of Education.

Another special education dispute activity tracked by CADRE is due process complaints, which are requests by parents of students with disabilities or school districts that their case be heard by an administrative hearing officer.

Whereas written state complaints ask a state agency to investigate a violation of special education law, due process complaints initiate a formal administrative hearing to resolve disputes over an individual student's identification, evaluation, placement or free and appropriate public education.

CADRE's analysis shows there were 23,656 due process complaints filed in 2024-25, a 40% drop compared to the year before. But, excluding New York's data, the analysis shows a 7% year-over-year increase in these complaints.

CADRE explained that in 2024-25, New York revised its procedures by establishing a separate Enhanced Rate Equitable Services unit and shifting certain cases out of the state’s IDEA due process system. That led to a decline of 16,389 reported due process filings in the state between 2023-24 and 2024-25, according to CADRE.

Still, New York, California and Puerto Rico made up more than 72% of the due process complaints filed nationally, CADRE said.

Another data point that CADRE points to — as a positive trend — is a continued increase in mediation requests to resolve special education disputes. The 2024-25 school year saw the highest number of mediation requests — 13,197 — to date. The 2024-25 school year also saw the highest number of mediations held at 7,336.

Mediation may be used anytime a family and a district disagree over special education services, including if there is a due process complaint. A school district official or a family can request mediation and the activity is voluntary for both parties.

Although the number of times such mediation requests successfully led to agreements slipped in 2024-25, mediations not tied to due process complaints reached agreement about two-thirds of the time. That "underscores the value of investing in early, collaborative approaches that help families and schools resolve concerns before they escalate," Reese said.

Special education dispute resolution experts point out that while conflict is natural, it can lead to negative impacts for students when it goes unresolved.

CADRE promotes other preventative and early dispute resolution approaches. For instance, it encourages schools to use facilitators at individualized education program meetings, if there is conflict, and to engage and partner with families of students with disabilities.

Community colleges drove the modest overall increase. They saw a year-over-year 1.2% bump in students, building on a 3.5% annual increase they experienced last fall.

"What was a relatively small decline last year has really become more, much more pronounced,” Holsapple said at the press conference.

The overall numbers mask considerable variance, including by fields of study.

Computer science programs experienced the biggest losses among subject areas in the clearinghouse’s data, with four-degree undergraduate enrollment plummeting 12.4% year over year this fall.

Amid tech layoffs and the take-off of automated coding, unemployment rates for recent computer science and computer engineering graduates reached 7% and 7.8%, respectively, in 2024, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. That's among the very highest rates of recent college grads unemployment by degree field and roughly 3 percentage points above the overall unemployment rate that year.

“Computer science led years of strong growth before reversing direction last fall, where we started to see numbers really drop,” Holsapple said. “This year, it's even more pronounced.”

Visual and performing arts, as well as liberal sciences, both saw enrollment drop by over 4% at the undergraduate level. In contrast, engineering and health professional degree enrollment each spiked by over 6%.

Western Maricopa Education Center’s competitive pay has helped limit teacher vacancies, says a leader at the career and technical education district.

Illustration: Cathryn Virginia for Industry Dive

Staffed Up is a monthly series examining school staffing best practices and solutions for teacher recruitment and retention.

Shrinking enrollment and teacher shortages in hard-to-fill roles are ongoing challenges in many school districts nationwide, but that’s not the case for Western Maricopa Education Center.

The Arizona public school district offers 43 career and technical education programs across six campuses in the nearly 3,900 square miles of Western Maricopa County, which is part of the Phoenix metropolitan area.

West-MEC partners with 15 public school districts and two charter districts to offer CTE courses to the districts’ high school students at those student's assigned schools. Those courses are taught in-house by the partnering district’s own staff. Partnering districts can also send their students to take CTE classes at one of six West-MEC campuses for 2.5 hours a day, said Ryan French, West-MEC’s assistant superintendent for business and operations.

The district offers a wide range of CTE programs, including in the fields of artificial intelligence, cosmetology, IT security, nursing, paralegal and veterinary science.

Student demand is surpassing the number of seats available for classes at West-MEC’s six campuses, which select its students through an application process, said French. West-MEC’s six campuses enrolled about 3,000 students this school year, he said, while 6,000 students typically apply every year for West-MEC’s CTE programs.

“We still have seen a significant demand for programs, and we’re trying to expand as fast as we can to help meet those needs,” French said.

West-MEC also employs about 100 CTE instructors on its campuses, French said, and there are almost zero vacancies for those roles this year. The district’s CTE teacher retention rate is around 90%, he added. As the district continues to increase its course offerings, he said, it plans to have filled an additional 18 CTE instructional roles by the 2027-28 school year.

The district is uniquely well-positioned compared to other schools nationwide, as federal data has shown persistent challenges with CTE staffing.

For instance, 31% of public schools reported difficulties with hiring for open CTE teaching positions during the 2020-21 school year, according to the most recently available survey data from the U.S. Department of Education’s National Center for Education Statistics. For the 2025-26 and 2026-27 school years, 29 states and the District of Columbia reported they experienced a CTE teacher shortage, Education Department data shows.

A common reason districts struggle to recruit and retain CTE instructors is that they can’t keep up with competitive industry salaries, French said. While West-MEC also cannot offer comparable industry wages to its CTE teachers, he said, the district is paying “significantly higher than a normal teaching wage.”

The average teacher salary at West-MEC was $78,823 in fiscal year 2025 compared to Arizona's average pay for all teachers of $65,613 in the same fiscal year. However, West-MEC’s average wages have since grown to $85,247 for FY27.

West-MEC receives supplemental state funds based on student enrollment to help cover its competitive teacher pay in addition to supporting its partnering districts with student counseling services and professional development, French said.

Arizona’s public education system, more broadly, is not getting a lot of support from the state as its legislature has pushed for school choice policies, such as the statewide universal school choice program known as Empowerment Scholarship Accounts, French said. Since FY20, Arizona’s public school enrollment has dropped 6.2% — or by 55,054 students — to 839,126 in FY25.

“School districts have a tough time right now, but somehow, some way, CTE kind of stands on its own right now,” French said. “We have a lot of support from both sides of the aisle when it comes to CTE.”

He added that West-MEC is trying to tap into that local public support for CTE and momentum as the district continues to expand. Last November, voters approved a $415 million bond to help the district build more campuses in high-demand fields like healthcare, technology and welding, particularly as student interest in its CTE programs surges.

It’s important to invest in staffing in order to have high-quality CTE programs, French said. For other school districts and states looking to boost their CTE enrollment, build out their programs and improve staffing, he advises those leaders to not “be afraid to think outside the box” and take advantage of the growing public support for CTE programs.

“If the answer to the question is ‘We’ve always done it that way,’ then you know you have some work to do,” French said, adding that school leaders can also look to other local and national innovative CTE programs. From there, he said, leaders can compare the differences and similarities their district may have with those programs — and “maybe you’re closer to that model than you think.”

The final rule, set to publish Friday in the Federal Register, will ensure accrediting agencies “prioritize diversity of thought on campuses,” the agency said.

But the Education Department asserted, “contrary to the hyperbole exercised by several commenters,” the new regulations do not force accreditors to adopt specific standards in exchange for federal recognition.

The university is taking campus comment on the plan, which is still in its recommendation phase, and will hold a series of listening sessions. Scholz will also meet with UO’s vice presidents in November before he makes a final decision on the proposal in early December.

That means focusing GE funding on doctoral training and research, where ongoing support “is central to the degree model,” it said.

Other programs are restricting their admissions to avoid doling out as much financial support for graduate students.

The university plan said it will honor the GE support to which it has already committed.

UO is also eyeing several organizational consolidations, including combining its Classics and Religious Studies departments. The merger would “eliminate duplicate leadership responsibilities and reduce faculty service and administrative demands,” the plan said.

The university would similarly combine its earth sciences and geography departments under the proposal. And it would merge its design college with its school of music and dance.

Several converging factors, most of which center on enrollment, have resulted in UO’s budget pinch, according to Scholz.

Oregon's high school graduate population is expected to drop about 19%, outpacing the national forecast of a 13% decrease according to data from the Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education.

Scholz cited increased competition with peer universities for students and a significant loss of out-of-state enrollment. Nearly half of UO’s students, 47.1%, are from outside of Oregon, a notable share for a public flagship. And the university’s tuition sticker price for out-of-state undergraduates is more than double when it lists for in-state students.

The university also faces a politically engineered drop in international enrollment.

“These dynamics are not only affecting the University of Oregon,” Scholz said. “They represent a changing reality across higher education and are likely to be the new market environment to which we must adjust.”

At the start of 2026, Oregon’s Higher Education Coordinating Commission said all seven public universities are facing shrinking pools of potential students, declining enrollment and constrained state funding.

#7056. Choosing digital curriculum that works for every learner

Today's classrooms are more diverse than ever. Students bring different strengths, abilities, backgrounds, interests and preferences to every learning experience. As digital learning continues to expand, curriculum providers face an important challenge: designing courses that meet the needs of all learners, not just some.

When evaluating digital curriculum, five pillars define course quality: standards alignment, audience-centered design, data-informed decisions, accessibility and continuous improvement.

One of the most revealing questions educators can ask during the evaluation process is simple: Who was this course designed for?

If the answer is "every learner," accessibility can't be an afterthought. It must be built into the curriculum from the start. Increasingly, schools are asking whether a course can truly be considered high quality if every learner cannot fully engage with it.

More than 8 million students in the United States receive special education services and many use assistive technologies as part of their everyday learning experience. Some may rely on screen readers, text magnification tools, speech-to-text software or adaptive devices. Others benefit from options that allow them to customize how they interact with content and demonstrate mastery.

Yet accessible design extends well beyond students with identified disabilities.

An English learner may benefit from listening to content while following along visually. A student recovering from a concussion may need alternatives to text-heavy materials. A student accessing coursework from multiple devices may benefit from responsive design and intuitive navigation. Features originally designed to improve accessibility often enhance the learning experience for all students.

In practice, accessible digital curriculum incorporates features that make learning more flexible and intuitive for a broad range of students, including:

Closed captioning and transcripts for multimedia content

Meaningful alternative text for images and graphics

Accessible color contrast and visual design

Descriptive links and navigational elements

Many of these practices align with the principles of Universal Design for Learning (UDL), which encourages educators to provide multiple ways for students to engage with content, process information and demonstrate understanding. Rather than designing for a single type of learner, UDL recognizes that flexibility benefits all students and should be incorporated into learning experiences from the beginning.

For district leaders, accessibility is also a strong indicator of a curriculum provider's overall commitment to quality. Providers that prioritize accessible design are often demonstrating a broader investment in learner-centered development, thoughtful user experience and continuous improvement.

Through these experiences, students gain firsthand insight into public procurement and the role it plays in serving communities.

Through internships, scholarships, career fairs, guest speakers, research collaborations and other opportunities, states can introduce students to public procurement while building relationships with institutions that can help develop the future workforce.

Together with NASPO Ignite and its other talent development initiatives, these efforts reflect NASPO’s continued commitment to helping students discover public procurement and giving state procurement offices more ways to connect with emerging talent.

Microsoft said its models must stay within the permissions and resources provided for a task and cannot independently expand their goals. They also must "never resist human interruption, override, correction, or shutdown."

That requirement extends to subagents. If a Microsoft model delegates work to another AI system, that system must inherit the same restrictions and respond to later instructions to stop or change course.

The document also addresses the increasingly blurry line between conversational software and human companionship. Under the heading "AI is Artificial," Microsoft says its models should not pretend to possess feelings, personal motivations, or subjective experiences. The company describes its AI as "not conscious" and rejects the idea that models should receive legal personhood, welfare protections, or rights.

That position is notably firmer than Anthropic's latest stance. In its updated constitution for Claude, Anthropic says it remains uncertain whether advanced models could possess consciousness or moral status. Still, the two companies share important ground: Both place human oversight above model independence and use written constitutions to guide training and behavior.

OpenAI is taking a similar path with its public Model Spec and safeguards for advanced AI. Much of that work is about keeping models under control as they become more capable, especially when they can handle cybersecurity tasks or work on their own.

For IT teams, Microsoft's code offers an early look at the rules that could shape future MAI-powered products. The models should admit when they aren't sure, avoid inventing sources, protect sensitive data, and keep records of what they do. They should also ask questions when they aren't sure they have permission to act.

Microsoft said the code describes where it wants to go, not how its models always work today. The written rules are only a starting point, and Microsoft will still need testing, monitoring, evaluations, and incident response to back them up. In addition, it's important to note that the code only covers models built by Microsoft AI: It doesn't apply to outside models Microsoft hosts or uses in its products, including those from OpenAI and Anthropic.

#7019. Security Researchers: Passkey Phishing Attacks Are Leading to Cloud Account Takeovers

In an active social engineering campaign, cyber attackers are impersonating IT help desks and using fake passkey setup requests to compromise employee identities and gain access to enterprise cloud data.

In a recent blog post, Microsoft Security Research said it has observed the activity since May 2026 across multiple compromised accounts.

The attacks have included unusual sign-ins, attacker-added authentication methods, high-volume Microsoft Graph activity, SharePoint and OneDrive downloads, and e-mail collection through REST APIs.

The attacks often begin with a phone call or message sent to an employee's personal phone.

The attacker poses as someone from the organization's IT help desk and tells the victim that a passkey, multifactor authentication (MFA), or single sign-on configuration needs to be updated to avoid disruption.

Victims are then directed to a website designed to resemble a legitimate Microsoft sign-in experience.

Microsoft has also observed links being delivered through SMS messages and, in some cases, through Microsoft Teams messages sent from already compromised employee accounts.

Despite the passkey-themed approach, the tech company stressed that enrolling a passkey is generally not the attacker's actual objective.

Instead, the request provides a pretext for directing victims through adversary-in-the-middle phishing or device-code authentication.

An adversary-in-the-middle attack can capture credentials and session tokens, while device-code phishing can trick a victim into authorizing access for an attacker-controlled client.

In one attack investigated by the tech giant, an anomalous sign-in from an unmanaged device was followed by access to identity and application management services.

The attacker then used SharePoint Online and OneDrive to enumerate sensitive files, primarily through Microsoft Graph. The sessions persisted for roughly an hour while the attacker searched for sensitive files and internal applications.

Gaining initial access isn't necessarily the end of the identity attack.

Microsoft found attackers registering new authentication methods, including phone numbers, authenticator apps, and software-based one-time password tokens, under their control.

That gives the attacker another way to satisfy future authentication challenges and helps turn an initial compromise into a more persistent foothold.

From there, attackers have used Microsoft Graph to map users, groups, permissions, applications, and accessible content across compromised tenants.

The firm says the activity can eventually progress to accessing mail, files, attachments, and other document content.

Microsoft attributed initial access activity associated with the campaign to multiple threat actors, including Storm-3121 and Storm-3032.

Storm-3121 conducts initial access activity that can lead to ShinyHunters and Falcon extortion, while Storm-3032 represents actors that split from the BlackFile group and now operate under the Helix extortion banner.

For administrators, Microsoft recommends enforcing phishing-resistant MFA such as FIDO2 passkeys and Windows Hello for Business through Conditional Access.

Organizations should also consider blocking device-code and authentication-transfer flows where they aren't required and investigate unusual sign-ins followed by new authentication methods, Graph reconnaissance, and abnormal SharePoint, OneDrive, or mailbox activity.

The campaign highlights an important distinction for organizations adopting passkeys: The attackers aren't necessarily defeating the technology. They're convincing employees that they need help setting it up, then using that trust to compromise their identities.
