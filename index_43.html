The Federal Reserve Board on Wednesday finalized changes to enhance the transparency and public accountability of its stress test and reduce volatility in its stress test-related capital requirements. The two final rules are largely similar to the proposed rules from 2025. The Board also requested comment on a proposal to better capture differences in banks' business models to generate fee income.

The Board conducts stress tests to ensure that large banks are sufficiently capitalized and able to lend to households and businesses even in a severe recession. In December 2024, the Board announced it would modify the test to improve its resilience.

"The stress test is an essential component of our regulatory capital framework," said Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle W. Bowman. "Today's changes preserve its resilience by ensuring that it is transparent, granular, and risk-sensitive. The public will now have greater assurance that the risks banks take will be reflected appropriately in their stress test losses and their capital requirements."

The first final rule requires the Board to invite public input annually on the stress test scenarios and any material model changes. It also updates the framework that guides the design of the hypothetical scenarios and adopts the models that will be used for the 2027 stress test. In addition, the final rule makes several adjustments to the stress test calendar. The final rule also updates the global market shock component, which is a set of shocks to specific market components applied to banks with large trading books. These banks will now be tested against two global market shock components each year, and the Board will use the shock that produces the largest losses for each firm to calculate the firm's stress test results.

The second final rule requires the Board, when calculating stress capital buffer requirements, to average the results from the two most recent annual supervisory stress tests for firms subject to the stress test in both years. #2988. Fed will vote on rates less often. What that could mean for borrowers

After the Federal Reserve held its benchmark interest rate steady last week, Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh announced plans to reduce the number of rate-setting votes over the next two years. That move could potentially extend the timeline for everyday Americans to see borrowing costs adjust, some experts say.

"Fewer Fed meetings in 2027 could mean consumers wait longer to see relief when borrowing costs are elevated," said James Vrachas, executive director at advisory firm Post Oak Group.

Americans may feel somewhat removed from the Fed, but the central bank's moves have a ripple effect on many types of consumer products, including the borrowing rates for credit cards, home loans and auto financing.

For example, most credit cards have variable rates tied to the prime rate, which moves with the Fed's benchmark. However, even with the central bank holding rates unchanged, credit card rates are edging higher as issuers protect themselves from risk, according to LendingTree.

Card issuers are mitigating their exposure from borrowers who may fall behind on payments or default in the face of stubborn inflation in everyday expenses, said Matt Schulz, LendingTree's chief credit analyst.

Generally, card issuers are slow to bring rates down, unless prompted by the Fed's actions, said Schulz: "Issuers will tinker with rates a bit at the margins at other times, but the big moves tend to only come when issuers are pushed."

On Oct. 28, the central bank voted to keep the benchmark borrowing rate at the current target range of 3.75% to 4% as policymakers deliberated next steps with inflation remaining well above the Fed's 2% goal.

The Fed will keep meeting eight times a year, Warsh said at the news conference following the October meeting, but next year, one of the eight meetings will not entail a vote on interest rates or include a post-meeting statement. The "strategy" meetings will increase to two the following year, leaving just six opportunities to adjust rates — with the exception of emergency meetings, if necessary.

"For consumers, that could mean longer periods of uncertainty around borrowing costs," said Jordan Ricciardi, a senior wealth strategy associate at UBS. "Our clients looking to buy a home, refinance a mortgage or finance a business could be impacted as fewer scheduled opportunities means the Fed isn't as quickly responding to changing economic conditions."

With fewer scheduled opportunities to adjust rates, that means borrowing rates could remain stubbornly high even if economic conditions begin to improve, Post Oak Group's Vrachas also said.

As it stands, financial institutions can adjust rates whenever market conditions warrant, regardless of the Fed's meeting schedule, he said. In the meantime, though, many consumers may continue to face elevated borrowing costs for mortgages, car loans and credit card debt, among other financial products.

"For consumers, the bigger concern isn't necessarily fewer meetings, but whether lenders pass along lower borrowing costs as quickly as they pass along increases," Vrachas said.

Consumers may be even more impacted by the lack of transparency, economists say, as Warsh implements measures to curtail so-called forward guidance, or how the Fed signals its future rate moves.

Although economic conditions have become more complicated and information is more widely available, "Warsh keeps his proverbial cards closer to his chest than his recent predecessors have," said Mark Hamrick, an economic analyst and founder of The Hamrick Brief, a weekly newsletter.

"I wouldn't expect consumers to see the universe of market rates change simply because the Fed votes less often," he said. "But the public may have less opportunity to hear from the source about how and why the Fed is making the decisions that shape what they pay to borrow and what they earn on their savings."

In the interim, "the best protection for consumers remains shopping around, particularly for the highest yields among savings accounts and [certificates of deposit]," Hamrick said.

As long as the Fed keeps rates unchanged, some top-yielding online savings accounts are offering interest rates as high as 4% or more, Bankrate data shows. "That's one of the upsides of an elevated interest rate environment: savers can earn meaningfully higher yields," Hamrick said.

#2986. Defense, healthcare, crypto and AI: How the midterm election could affect stocks

Lockheed Martin gets about 70% of its sales from the U.S. government. Medicaid accounts for three-quarters of Molina Healthcare's premium revenue. Coinbase is waiting on Congress to finish writing the rules for cryptocurrency.

For some of the biggest U.S. companies, Washington is part of the business model. Now, the midterm election could change who holds the leverage.

A Democratic victory in either or both chambers of Congress would grant the party new leverage to investigate companies, challenge federal spending and demand legislative concessions. But their reach would have limits: President Donald Trump would retain his veto and broad authority over tariffs, immigration and federal regulation.

Historically, stocks have performed well under divided government. Under Republican presidents, the S&P 500 has gained an average of 23% in the year after midterms when Congress was split, compared with 16% when Democrats controlled both chambers, according to a Morgan Stanley analysis published Thursday. Analysts often attribute the pattern in part to congressional gridlock limiting sweeping policy changes.

Still, some analysts caution against assuming that pattern will repeat.

"We don't think that blueprint holds up this time around, and we still see plenty of reasons to be constructive on the U.S. equity market," said Ariana Salvatore, head of U.S. public policy research at Morgan Stanley.

For investors, the midterm election could reshape which businesses preserve their advantages in Washington, particularly in industries such as defense, healthcare, banking and artificial intelligence.

"There are some policy changes that might be market moving, at least for certain segments," said Jeff Buchbinder, chief equity strategist at LPL Financial.

Control of either the House or Senate would give Democrats committee gavels and subpoena power, allowing them to demand documents, compel testimony and haul executives before public hearings.

On Oct. 21, Sen. Richard Blumenthal, D-Conn., the top Democrat on the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, sought records tied to Trump's White House ballroom from donors including Cisco, Salesforce, ServiceNow and Kraken. Apple and Nvidia each committed $25 million to the project, according to records obtained by The Washington Post.

Rep. Jamie Raskin, D-Md., has separately sought records involving tariff exclusions, including communications involving Apple's former CEO, Tim Cook, as Democrats investigate whether Trump used trade policy to reward political allies and donors.

While donations do not prove wrongdoing or favorable treatment, congressional probes still carry risks: subpoenas consume executive bandwidth, reveal internal communications and generate unpredictable news coverage. Few CEOs relish the idea of sitting at a witness table before lawmakers and television cameras.

"It creates volatility because you get a negative headline," said Monica Guerra, Morgan Stanley's head of policy and geopolitical strategy, who expects Democrats to cast a wide net for their probes.

Democrats have signaled interest in Trump family businesses, government contractors, firms tied to Donald Trump Jr.'s 1789 Capital, and companies associated with Elon Musk and the so-called Department of Government Efficiency.

Then there is the most basic power Congress has: deciding where the money goes.

Trump's 2025 tax cuts and spending law, known as One Big Beautiful Bill, is projected to reduce federal Medicaid spending by $911 billion through 2034, according to a KFF analysis of Congressional Budget Office estimates. KFF is a nonpartisan nonprofit that provides health policy information. Meanwhile, new work requirements for Medicaid enrollees are scheduled to take effect Jan. 1, or earlier in some states, while tighter state budgets could mean fewer people are covered and greater pressure on provider payments.

Molina Healthcare generated $32.2 billion, or 75% of its 2025 premium revenue, from Medicaid, according to the company's annual Securities and Exchange Commission filing. In the same filing, Molina warned that losing government contracts or failing to renew them on favorable terms could materially reduce premium revenue.

A Democratic-controlled chamber could not repeal the 2025 law on its own but could use must-pass budget negotiations to seek funding changes or delays to some cuts.

"They're probably going to push back on those Medicaid cuts," Buchbinder told CNBC, noting that preserving coverage could help stabilize enrollment for Medicaid insurers and patient volumes for hospitals.

Hospitals face their own squeeze: fewer insured patients can mean more uncompensated care, while state budget pressure can reduce reimbursement rates, according to KFF. Congress created a $50 billion rural health fund over five years to cushion some of the blow, but KFF and medical providers have said the money would not fully offset the broader Medicaid reductions.

Democratic gains could still cut both ways for healthcare. The same lawmakers seeking to protect Medicaid funding could also intensify scrutiny of insurers, hospitals and drugmakers over prices, coverage and profits.

A Democratic Congress could challenge some Iran- and Israel-related spending while pushing for more aid to Ukraine. However, bipartisan consensus on China deterrence, nuclear modernization and missile defense could protect other major programs, according to multiple analysts.

Lockheed Martin, for example, recently reached a framework agreement with the Pentagon covering 850 F-35 fighter jets over a decade. But the agreement is not yet a finalized contract: Congress must authorize the multiyear purchase and provide the funding.

"In some ways, the industry doesn't really care who got elected," said Sam Stovall, chief investment strategist at CFRA Research. "They just want to know what the rules are going to be for the coming year."

Federal regulators estimate their proposed capital overhaul would cut requirements for the largest banks by roughly 4.8%, potentially freeing billions for lending and shareholder payouts. The Federal Reserve writes the rules, but Senate control shapes who fills future vacancies, and congressional committees can pressure regulators through hearings and investigations.

"It's at the regulatory level where stuff can happen that matters," Buchbinder said.

The midterms therefore could decide who gets the power. Democratic gains could put more grid-upgrade costs on tech developers, raising project costs just as access to power is constraining new construction.

"We think the election can have meaningful consequences, but on a highly localized basis," Salvatore said.

#2962. Feeling isolated? Half of in-office workers describe their desk jobs as 'remote,' NY Fed survey finds

Many workers across the U.S. are now back in-office a majority of the time, and they are feeling more isolated than ever.

A recent survey from researchers at the New York Fed finds that roughly half of workers based in-office don't describe a daily experience on the job that matches the goal many corporate employers stated in upping in-person mandates.

Whether three-, four- or five-day mandates, companies have cited the importance of creative collaboration for business productivity and growth, as well as mentorship opportunities. Yet workers surveyed by the NY Fed describe endless Zoom calls and Slacks, and little actual face time.

"Days in the office often lack human contact," the researchers write. Specifically, 48.8% of survey respondents said they had no in-person meetings, either with teammates or non-teammates, on the days they were most recently in the office.

There are a variety of reasons for the disconnect between in-office work mandates and actual experience.

Roughly one in four workers are now fully remote, according to U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data cited by the NY Fed, but they say that alone likely "understates the explosion in the experience of remoteness from colleagues."

With teams being spread across more offices — an effect they say has become more pronounced in the post-Covid organizational structure — many workers "no-showing" on mandates, and employers not enforcing mandates strictly, the result for in-office workers, according to the researchers, is that "they commute in just to take Zoom calls and answer Slack messages from their cubicles."

The NY Fed paper, by NY Fed research economist Natalia Emanuel and University of Virginia economics professor Emma Harrington, concludes that "a staggering share of the workforce experiences this form of in-office remote work."

The survey was conducted in September among a final sample of 1,835 college-educated workers. The researchers chose September, they say, to generate a "conservative estimate," since many companies push in-office attendance after summer ends, and the period between Labor Day and Thanksgiving has less remote work than holiday periods.

The survey found that on in-office days, 70.6% of workers had at least one teammate who was not in the office; while half had no in-person meetings with all participants present, and over a third had neither in-person or hybrid meetings.

"Amazingly, 37% of respondents reported that they had at least one meeting that was virtual, even though all the participants were in the same office that day. In short, many workers' commutes are simply yielding a new background for their Zoom calls," they write.

They cite the example of Zoom CEO Eric Yuan, who said in 2023, "On Zoom, it's really hard. We cannot have a great conversation. We cannot debate each other well."

But the researchers conclude, "if the debates in the office are also happening over Zoom, the benefits of the office may not be so large."

The authors say that separate information collected on a Fortune 500 company employee base to which they had personnel data and badge swipe data shows that nearly half of corporate employees have at least one teammate who is out of the office on their in-office days. They say additional data from the BLS's American Time Use Survey similarly show that "Americans now spend more time physically alone on days they go into the office than they did before the pandemic."

Other recent polling data from Gallup found worker sentiment to be at a 15-year low.

The NY Fed authors caution that their research does not capture relationships with non-teammates, or worker's in-person interactions with clients, often outside the office. They also stress that workers who come to the office often cite seeing colleagues as a major draw. "But since the rise of remote work, the office increasingly fails to deliver this benefit," they write.

They contend that the research has practical implications for both employers and employees.

For one, the experience of remote work is much more widespread than understood, which could have many repercussions not being dealt with effectively now, such as mental health issues.

Second, organizational structure post-pandemic may not be optimal for making in-office mandates work. It may, in fact, just lead to more pushback, they write.

But the authors do not conclude employers should give up on the mandates. To the contrary, they write, "ensuring adherence to the policy helps deliver the face-to-face contact that workers crave."

A credit spread is the difference in the yield between the corporate bonds and Treasurys of similar maturities. When spreads tighten, it means investors are becoming more confident about the bond issuer.

The market action over the last few days shows that investors are recognizing that "credit fears may have been overblown," said CNBC contributor Paul Meeks, head of technology research at Freedom Capital Markets.

"It's not necessarily fundamentals," said CreditSights analyst Jordan Chalfin. "It's more, I'd say, 80% driven by technicals and sentiment."

S&P Global Ratings estimated in an August report that the hyperscalers will have negative free operating cash flow in 2026 and 2027.

#2933. I bonds now pay 5.51% — but there are trade-offs. What to know before buying

Now that Series I bonds pay higher interest, the government-backed, nearly risk-free asset could be more attractive for certain investors. But I bonds have downsides, and there are alternatives to consider.

Newly purchased I bonds currently pay 5.51% annual interest through April 30, up from the 4.26% yield offered on purchases made through October.

Amid rising inflation, the variable part of I bond interest rose to 4.08%, while the fixed part increased to 1.40%, the highest since 2006. Combined, the 5.51% composite rate is the biggest yield since November 2022.

The new I bond rate is "very attractive," according to Ken Tumin, founder of DepositQuest.com, a blog that tracks I bond rates, among other deposit accounts.

The variable part of I bond interest resets every six months, based on the consumer price index. For the next six months after purchase, I bonds look "very competitive" compared to other shorter-term options for cash, such as savings accounts or certificates of deposit, according to Tumin.

Meanwhile, the I bond's 1.4% fixed rate stays the same for as long as you own it, which could appeal to longer-term investors, he said.

But I bonds aren't right for everyone. Here are some key things to know before purchasing.

Investors can buy $10,000 of electronic I bonds per person per calendar year via their TreasuryDirect account. You can open additional TreasuryDirect accounts for other family members or businesses to buy more.

I bond interest is tax-deferred until redemption and is not subject to state or local taxes. You can also avoid federal taxes if you use the money for qualified education expenses.

But experts say there are some downsides. The funds are locked up for 12 months from purchase, and you'll lose your last three months of interest if you redeem within five years.

Advisors typically recommend keeping at least three to six months of living expenses in cash for emergency savings. I bonds could boost those reserves, experts say, as long as you keep enough readily accessible cash elsewhere.

"I always say [I bonds are] a secondary emergency fund," said David Enna, founder of Tipswatch.com, a website that tracks Treasury inflation-protected securities, or TIPS, and I bond rates.

Once you own I bonds for five years, you can redeem the money as needed, penalty-free.

With I bond purchase limits, restrictions and some TreasuryDirect complaints, advisors often recommend other options for clients who want to preserve purchasing power.

"For most of my clients, I bonds are more hassle than they're worth," said certified financial planner Eileen Olson, founder of advisory firm Emerald Wealth in Daufuskie Island, South Carolina. "The amount is too small to matter."

On a $2 million portfolio, the $10,000 annual purchase limit is only 0.5%, and it earns about $550 in a year before federal taxes, she said. "That won't protect anyone's lifestyle from inflation."

Instead, Olson prefers TIPS, which don't have purchase limits and can be bought alongside other assets, outside TreasuryDirect.

TIPS have five-, 10- and 30-year terms, with yields ranging from about 2.3% to above 3% for the 30-year term, as of Nov. 2.

However, the right option depends on your investing goals and timeline, experts say.

"For short-term cash, Treasury bills and high-yield savings are simpler," Olson said.

Also backed by the U.S. government, Treasury bills, or T-bills, have terms ranging from four weeks up to 52 weeks. As of Nov. 2, some T-bill yields were well over 4%.

By comparison, the top 1% average of high-yield savings accounts were paying above 4%, according to rate comparison site DepositAccounts. Some one-year certificates of deposit were also offering rates over 4%, the same day, according to Bankrate.

Money market funds are another option. Some of the biggest funds were paying around 4%, as of Nov. 2, according to Crane Data.

Intel's story took a dramatic turn for the worse on Thursday, when the company said revenue this quarter will be between $1 billion and $2 billion shy of analysts' estimates as the PC market continues to suffer from soaring costs for memory and other components. The stock plummeted 29% on Friday, its worst day since the 1970s, capping off a 45% plunge in October, the steepest monthly drop on record, according to FactSet. It's down another 8% in Monday trading.

#2881. Stock market after midterm elections: What S&P 500 history shows

Markets are sweating it out ahead of the midterm elections, but the event historically heralds strong performance for stocks — regardless of whether it's a red or blue wave, split decision, or status quo in Washington.

The S&P 500 tends to perform well after both midterm and presidential elections. According to a CNBC analysis of S&P 500 returns after elections from 1980 through 2024, the index rose an average of 0.2% after one day and one week, and 1.1% after one month across both presidential and midterm elections.

A year later, the index gained an average of 13.7% after midterms and 13.8% after presidential elections. Returns were positive in 91% of cases. And the S&P 500 was up after all 11 midterms from 1982 through 2022.

Short-term returns were less reliable, particularly after presidential contests, which produced slightly negative average returns after one day and one week.

"There's some anxiousness before these elections," Ryan Detrick, CNBC contributor and chief market strategist at Carson Group, told CNBC. "Once you are through it, it's a big relief and markets tend to do pretty well."

Still, the market usually prefers divided government: one party in charge of the White House and the other holding at least one house of Congress.

Across 10 midterm and five presidential elections that left no single party in charge, the S&P 500 rose an average 15% after one year. One-year returns were positive in 14 out of 15 cases. By comparison, the index gained an average of 11.5% over the year after elections that resulted in single-party control.

"If you have a huge red wave or a big blue wave, markets actually kind of don't like that as much because you have too much spending one way and too much power," Detrick said.

Leaving Democrats in control of Congress with a Republican in the White House — a scenario that may emerge from the elections on Tuesday — was also positive. A CNBC analysis found that after the three midterms (1986, 1990 and 2006) when Democrats controlled both houses and a Republican held the presidency, the S&P 500 averaged a 0.5% decline on the reaction day but was up 3.4% a month later and 10.9% after a year.

Granted, elections may be coincident events to factors that matter far more for market performance.

The S&P 500 plunged 22.1% in the year after the 2000 elections due to the dotcom bust, marking the worst post-election performance since 1980. Conversely, the S&P 500's best post-election year was a 38.3% gain after the 2020 elections, when markets mounted a massive comeback from the COVID-induced selloff.

Investors should bear in mind that Federal Reserve policy and the corporate earnings cycle ultimately matter more than elections, according to Société Générale.

Stocks declined in the aftermath of the 2018 midterms, for instance, when Democrats wrested control of the House during President Donald Trump's first term. More importantly, the Fed tightened monetary policy that year, raising interest rates four times, and the S&P 500 ended the year down 6.2%, though the market was up 11.7% a year after the election.

The big swing factor now is AI and whether declines in tech will continue. As long as the AI wave is still moving and the economy keeps growing, "this bull market is probably still alive and well," Detrick says.

But a Democratic sweep of the House and Senate would almost certainly pressure the AI infrastructure buildout and could lead to more retrenchment.

The bond market is also skittish with yields rising globally and a mini debt crisis in France. And the war with Iran could get worse post-election if President Trump follows through on threats of more strikes or Iran escalates.

"Seasonality isn't destiny, and worries around Iran, AI and France won't mysteriously vanish," said Jim Reid, Deutsche Bank's global head of macro and thematic research, in a note.

If history is any guide, we could see a Santa rally and S&P 500 gains after the midterms. But this is still an AI-driven market, with geopolitics, the Fed, and bond market very much in charge.

Depending on the location, ballot measures are up for approval that would either provide tax breaks, or raise taxes on the wealthy.

"It's a complete bifurcation between red and blue states," said Dan Close, head of municipals at Nuveen. "Blue states are trying to put in place graduated income tax structures, trying to capture more revenue from high-income earners. Red states are promoting affordability by trying to cut property taxes."

Red states are those that predominantly vote Republican, while blue states swing toward Democrats.

For instance, California has a proposition known as the "billionaire tax," which would impose a one-time levy equal to 5% of the wealth of certain taxpayers with at least $1 billion in assets. The state also has a measure on the ballot that would make a temporary tax hike permanent on individuals earning over $371,000, adjusted annually for inflation. The tax is currently set to expire in 2031.

"The impact to the muni bond market will be to make the exemption more valuable," Close said of the possible passage of tax increases. Investors will likely seek shelter in the muni market, which would send bond prices higher, he noted.

It is a different story in Florida, where voters are set to decide whether to increase the Sunshine State's homestead exemption for non-school district property taxes. Qualified homeowners currently have a $25,000 property tax exemption, as well as another for $25,000 that doesn't apply to school property taxes. The new proposal raises the latter exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 by 2028. New residents have a five-year waiting period before receiving the benefit.

If passed, some local governments will have to find other revenue sources or increase the overall tax rate to replace what they've lost, said Tom Kozlik, head of public policy and municipal strategy at HilltopSecurities.

"It's not necessarily going to result in downgrades for sure, but there would have to be some balancing with regard to overall revenues," he said.

Not all municipalities will be hurt equally, which means credit research is important, said Close.

"Like everything in municipal finance, it is really on a case-by-case basis that you could have some credit deterioration from [a] rollback of property taxes," he said.

Other voters on Tuesday will be asked to approve more state or local government debt. That could weigh on a market that has already seen heightened supply.

Some $473.8 billion has already been issued year to date, through September, according to the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association. That's a 7.8% increase over the same period a year ago. In 2025, total muni issuance reached $587.3 billion, 14.3% higher than 2024, Sifma said.

Cooper Howard, director of fixed income research and strategy at the Schwab Center for Financial Research, believes supply is the biggest issue coming out of the election.

"That amount of issuance has been one of the reasons why you have to see that strong amount of demand continue to keep pace with it for total returns to keep up," Howard said.

Fortunately, new supply doesn't necessarily come quickly into the market. "There's a window over a couple years that they can issue," he noted.

The ultimate makeup of the next Congress could also have consequences for muni investors as lawmakers sort out their priorities. Most notable is what lawmakers will do to tackle the country's affordability problem, Kozlik at Hilltop said.
